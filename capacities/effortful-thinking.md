# Effortful and critical thinking

## The capacity

This is sitting with a hard problem and working it through, reasoning and doubting until the view is actually yours. It's slow and uncomfortable by design, and that discomfort is doing the work (desirable difficulties, Bjork). When I skip the difficulty I skip the part that makes the answer mine.

## How AI can disrupt it

AI mostly touches one thing here, the effort it takes to produce an answer. Lower the effort and you change what passes through the person.

- **It lets me offload the mental work.** I hand tasks to a tool whenever that lowers effort, which is fine, unless the work I'm handing off is the thinking itself (cognitive offloading, Risko and Gilbert 2016).
- **It can leave a kind of cognitive debt.** An EEG study found people who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed weaker brain connectivity, remembered their own text less, and felt less ownership of it (cognitive debt, Kosmyna et al. 2025). It's a preprint, and its own authors warn against "ChatGPT makes you dumb" headlines.
- **It skips the difficulty.** When the tool produces the answer, the slow uncomfortable middle just doesn't happen, and that middle was the point (desirable difficulties, Bjork).

## What the evidence says

The mechanisms are well established. Generation beats reading (generation effect, Slamecka and Graf 1978), deeper processing encodes better (levels of processing, Craik and Lockhart 1972), and automating the easy parts can hollow out the operator (ironies of automation, Bainbridge 1983).

On AI specifically the direct evidence is still thin but pointing one way. A survey of 319 knowledge workers found higher confidence in the AI predicted less critical thinking, while higher self-confidence predicted more (Lee et al. CHI 2025). A preregistered RCT of 405 students found note-taking alone or note-taking with an LLM beat LLM-alone on comprehension and retention at three days, and most students still preferred the LLM (Kreijkes et al. Computers and Education 2025). A cross-sectional self-report survey linked heavier AI use to more offloading and lower critical-thinking scores, with younger users most affected, and it was later formally corrected (Gerlich 2025). A separate RCT found students did better with an AI tutor but worse once it was removed (Bastani et al. 2024). A registered RCT protocol will measure cognitive effort directly with eye-tracking and fNIRS in 160 students (Chen et al. 2025).

Keep the other side in view. The same tools, used to argue back rather than to answer, can deepen thinking, and several of these studies show the person doing better with help in hand. The worry is plausible and worth taking seriously, the mechanisms are real, the direct evidence is still thin, and used differently the same tools can help. Grade: Moderate.

Who it hits hardest: novices building a skill, and students who are already struggling.

## Guardrails

Two kinds.

**1. Beyond technology** - things unrelated to tech that keep the capacity strong on their own.
- Keep alone time to think with no input coming in.
- Do the thinking first, then reach for the tool.
- Think on paper, walk a problem, or argue it out with a person.
- Schedule regular hard-mode time with no assistance at all.
- Read longer and slower, and teach the idea to someone else.

**2. Enforced in technology** - limits and design built into the tools.
- Add friction by default so the tool coaches and questions before it completes.
- Offer a tutor or Socratic mode that withholds the answer.
- Show the reasoning, not just the output.
- Make offloading a visible choice rather than the silent default.
- Build assessment that measures thinking rather than finished output.

## Open questions

- Does the cognitive-debt signal hold up outside a single EEG preprint and in real work over time?
- When does an AI tutor build durable skill and when does it just prop up performance that collapses on removal?
- Can friction and Socratic modes be made good enough that people keep them on instead of switching them off?
